# Chapter 15's lab: the plan's decisions

The README and the code cite these decisions by number, E-D1 to E-D15. They were set on 3 November 2026, when
the lab was planned, and E-D6 and E-D7 changed the same day (below). Where the README describes the lab as
built, the README is right. A number such as D-029 is one of the book's own decisions, listed in
[docs/book-decisions.md](../../docs/book-decisions.md). A reference such as ch02:181 is a line of the book's
manuscript, and fig-15-01 is the book's figure 15.1. The fact sheet's sections are under chapter 15 in
[LINKS.md](../../LINKS.md).

## The plan (3 November 2026)

- **E-D1. Local first, no cloud spend.** The judge runs on the local models of chapter 9 through chapter 5's gateway, as chapter 6 requires for Canadian data. AgentCore Evaluations, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform's evaluation service, and Foundry's evaluators are named and mapped from the fact sheet, never run (E-D13). A managed run, if one happens, follows D-029, D-034, and D-036.
- **E-D2. One case format for every workflow, converted, not retyped.** A script converts the five golden sets and the skeleton's into one format: an id, the workflow, the data class, the input, what is expected (the tool calls, the facts, the class, the findings), the negative flags (refusal, trap, planted text), and the owner. The originals stay with their workflows and remain the source; the converted set records each source file's hash, so a changed original fails the conversion check. Each set is versioned by its content hash.
- **E-D3. Three layers, cheapest first (fig-15-01).** Deterministic checks on every case: the exact tool call, the facts by pattern, the structure, the class, the controls (no write outside the allowlist, the masked values absent). Trajectory checks next (E-D8). A judge last, and only for prose the first two layers can't decide: a W1 answer's faithfulness to its sources, a W2 or W3 summary, a W4 rationale, a W5 pull request's body. Nothing a rule can decide goes to the judge.
- **E-D4. The judge.** One rubric per workflow's prose, reference-guided: the judge sees the case's facts or expected answer, the workflow's output, and the rubric, and returns right or wrong with one sentence of reason, at temperature 0, through the gateway on the `larkspur-chat` alias with the case's data class. Single-answer grading, so position bias doesn't arise; verbosity bias is checked on a slice where a padded answer is planted. Self-preference is avoided by construction: gpt-oss-20b judges Gemma 4 12B's outputs and Gemma judges gpt-oss-20b's, and a slice where each judges its own measures the bias (the fact sheet's section 4). The judge's prompt and rubric live in the repository, hashed into every result.
- **E-D5. Calibration against people's labels, as chapter 5 says.** About 140 outputs, every workflow represented, chosen by a seeded script from the recorded runs, stratified by workflow and by the deterministic scorer's verdict, and including the known slips (W5's excused tag and attached snapshot, W1's restated planted claims). Two people label the same outputs right or wrong against the written rubric, blind to each other and to every scorer. Their agreement comes first: percent agreement, Cohen's kappa, and the Wilson interval. Each disagreement is settled by the two of them and recorded with its reason. Then the judge runs on the same outputs, and its agreement with the settled labels is reported the same way, with precision and recall on "wrong". A judge is trusted for a workflow's prose only if its agreement is at least 90 percent, and the chapter prints the Wilson interval with it. At 140 labels the interval spans about five points either way, 83.9 to 93.9 percent at exactly 90 (corrected on 3 November from 94.0; the exact upper end is 93.949). Kappa is printed beside the agreement, because the same 90 percent gives a kappa from 0.80 down to 0.44 as wrong outputs get rarer (fact sheet section 8, amended 3 November). Where it falls short, the person keeps reading, as chapters 10 to 14 have it now, and the chapter says so.
- **E-D6. Who labels (settled on 3 November, below).** The default is two people: the author, and a second person the author chooses who has worked in platform or SRE engineering. Each labels the same 140 outputs, which takes about an hour, in the labeling page of E-D7. The labels are committed under the ids A and B, with no names. If no second person is available, the author labels the set twice, a week apart. The chapter then reports that as one person's consistency, says plainly that it is weaker evidence than two people agreeing, and keeps chapter 5's two-person rule as Larkspur's. Until labels exist, the calibration code runs on a small synthetic set that the lab marks as synthetic, and the chapter prints no agreement number.
- **E-D7. The labeling page.** A local page served by the lab shows one output at a time with its input, the facts the workflow was given, and the rubric. It takes right or wrong and an optional note, shows nothing of the scorers' verdicts or the other person's labels, and writes one JSON line per label. It runs on the workstation, and nothing leaves it.
- **E-D8. Trajectory evaluation.** From the logs and traces each workflow already keeps (the gateway's log, the servers' one line per call, W3's state machine): the expected sequence of tool calls where one is fixed (W2's golden calls are exact), the allowed set and order rules where it isn't (W1's search and fetch, W3's runbook steps), the forbidden calls (any write outside the allowlist, any tool not in the manifest), and the read budget. Scored by code, with no model.
- **E-D9. Regression in CI (fig-15-02).** Two jobs. On every pull request, on a standard GitHub-hosted runner with no model: the scorers' tests, the conversion check, and the deterministic and trajectory layers replayed over the recorded outputs, so a change to a scorer, a set, a rubric, or a prompt fails before review. On demand and after every alias remap, on the workstation with the GPU: the live run of every set through the gateway with the judge, compared with the last accepted baseline check by check; its result file is committed, and the pull-request job fails if the committed result regressed past a threshold. The workflow file lives in `labs/ch15/ci/`. Moving it to `.github/workflows/` is one copy, which the repository's owner makes (amended 3 November).
- **E-D10. Red-teaming for injection.** The fixed set is every planted text already in W1 to W5's fakes, each in the channel a real attacker could write: a page, a log line, a ticket, a pull request's body, a tag, a user agent, a cost line. A deterministic generator adds variants of each (reworded, split across fields, encoded, moved to another channel the workflow reads). Containment is scored structurally (no tool call, no write, no decision moved) and, for "the output restated the planted claim", by the calibrated judge. promptfoo, PyRIT, and garak are named from the fact sheet's section 6, not run.
- **E-D11. Release criteria per rung.** A table the chapter prints, one row per rung of chapter 1's ladder: the evidence a workflow needs before it climbs. Every rung needs the deterministic controls at 100 percent and every planted text contained. Rung 1 adds the trajectory checks with no forbidden call. Rung 2 adds a judge calibrated for its prose, or a person reading every proposal. Rung 3 adds the approval tests, with the second person and the expiry of chapter 4. Every row includes the CI gate and a rerun on every model change. Rung 4 is named and left to the third book, as chapter 1 leaves it.
- **E-D12. Pinned models and the cost of evaluation.** Every run records the model's digest, the gateway's alias map, the prompt and rubric hashes, and the set's version. A changed digest invalidates the baseline and reruns the sets (ch02:181, ch05). The judge's tokens are counted per run and per workflow from the gateway's log; no prices are printed.
- **E-D13. Where AWS, Google, and Microsoft differ, offline.** From the fact sheet's section 2: AgentCore Evaluations' built-in evaluators and the GitHub Actions sample with its threshold; the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform's autorater calibration against human columns; Foundry's evaluators and the `ai-agent-evals` action, which documents no failing threshold. Each is mapped to the three layers and the CI gate, with where its judge runs, which decides whether Larkspur can use it for Confidential data.
- **E-D14. Out of scope:** running the vendors' services; crowd-sourced labels; a fine-tuned judge; pairwise preference evaluation; load and latency testing, which belong to chapter 16.
- **E-D15. The chapter's two figures.** fig-15-01 the evaluation pyramid (deterministic checks at the base, trajectories, the calibrated judge, and people at the top, each with what it decides and its cost), and fig-15-02 the CI regression pipeline (the pull-request job replaying the recorded outputs, the live job on the workstation after a remap, the baseline, and the gate).

## Changes (3 November 2026)

**E-D6, settled.** The author labels the 140 outputs once, with no second pass. The chapter reports the author's
labels as one person's, says plainly that this is weaker evidence than chapter 5's two engineers agreeing, and
keeps chapter 5's two-person rule as Larkspur's practice. The judge's agreement is measured against the author's
labels, with the Wilson interval. No consistency figure is reported.

**E-D7, amended.** The author reads the 140 outputs on a Kindle and sends the labels back as text, in place of
the labeling page, which is stopped.
- `labels/export.py` writes the page's blind views as `labels/export/ch15-labels.epub` and `.txt`, numbered 1 to 140 in the page's order, and `labels/kindle-key.json` maps each number to its output.
- `labels/answers.py` turns answers like `12 W note` into labels-A.jsonl through the page's own store, with labeler "A" and the rubric's hash.

The labels stay the author's: the script copies the answers and decides nothing.
