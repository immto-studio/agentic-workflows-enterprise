# Chapter 16's lab: the plan's decisions

The README and the code cite these decisions by number, O-D1 to O-D18. They were set on 3 November 2026, when
the lab was planned. The same day the fact sheet's addendum amended twelve of them, and the cost ledger's first
results settled two more questions (below). Where the README describes the lab as built, the README is right. A
number such as D-029 is one of the book's own decisions, listed in
[docs/book-decisions.md](../../docs/book-decisions.md). A reference such as ch02:144 is a line of the book's
manuscript, and fig-16-01 is the book's figure 16.1. The fact sheet's sections, section 8 among them, are under
chapter 16 in [LINKS.md](../../LINKS.md).

## The plan (3 November 2026)

- **O-D1. Local first, one cloud session.** Whatever doesn't need a cloud runs on the workstation, over the kept runs and chapter 9's local models, as in chapter 15's lab. One AWS session (step 7), in us-east-2 on synthetic data (D-034, D-036), checks what only a cloud can show: spans in CloudWatch through Transaction Search, the trace id in Bedrock's model invocation log and in CloudTrail, how soon the deny-all policy takes effect, and Langfuse. The budget goes first, the plan state is read before the apply (D-029), and everything is destroyed in the same session and confirmed with `leftovers.py`. Google's and Microsoft's sides come from the fact sheet (O-D16).
- **O-D2. The trace identifier, joined both ways:** from a run to every record of it, and from a cloud audit event back to its run. The harness, the tool servers, and the gateway carry it already (chapter 5), so two links are new. The gateway passes `trace_id` and `workflow` in Bedrock's `requestMetadata`, which the model invocation log records and the bill never shows. The tool server adds the trace id as a session tag when it assumes a tool role, beside chapter 6's source identity and `workflow` tag. The role's trust policy requires the tag, so no tool-role session exists without one. CloudTrail records the tag on the AssumeRole event, and every later call made with those credentials carries the access key ID that event returned. `join.py` takes a trace id and prints every record of the run, from the harness and tool server logs to the gateway's cost lines, the model invocation log, and CloudTrail; given a CloudTrail event, it prints the run behind it. Chapter 6's trust policy gains the tag key, a companion change with tests, and chapter 16 tells it as a change Larkspur made.
- **O-D3. One pipeline with a collector in the middle.** Each process keeps the single OTLP exporter chapter 5 gave it and sends to an OpenTelemetry Collector (the contrib build, pinned, run from its Windows binary). The collector drops the content attributes a manifest doesn't allow (`trace_content: false`). Its tail sampling keeps every failed, refused, slow, over-budget, and fallback run and a share of the rest. It exports to the JSON lines file, to CloudWatch over OTLP with SigV4, and to Langfuse over OTLP/HTTP, since Langfuse takes no gRPC. The audit record and the cost lines never pass through sampling (ch03:58).
- **O-D4. Langfuse as the open backend.** Chapters 3 and 5 chose it. It needs PostgreSQL, ClickHouse, Redis, and S3-compatible storage. ClickHouse has no Windows build, and this workstation runs neither WSL2 nor Docker, so Langfuse runs from its Docker Compose file on one VM during the cloud session, reached through an EC2 Instance Connect endpoint with no port open to the internet, at a version pinned in step 1. The lab checks what Langfuse makes of the lab's spans: the trace tree, the token counts, and the cost, which Langfuse reads from `gen_ai.usage.cost` or computes from its own price table, where a self-hosted model has no price unless someone enters one. If the free plan's instance types can't run Langfuse, Phoenix runs on the workstation instead (pip, SQLite), and the chapter gives Langfuse's side from its documentation and says so.
- **O-D5. Cost per run comes from the cost lines.** A run's cost is the sum of its gateway cost lines, joined by trace id, each priced at the deployment that served it, a fallback at its own Region's price. The cost lines are never sampled, while a trace may be; the lab measures how far a workflow's month, estimated from traces sampled at 1, 10, and 50 percent, drifts from the cost lines' total. Prices come from one dated table built from the cost sheets of chapters 6 to 9 (`ledger/prices.yaml`, each price with its source and date) and load into the gateway's deployments as custom prices. A test sets the table against LiteLLM's built-in price map and lists every difference. The first to check is the Canada profile, which chapter 6 says bills at ca-central-1's price, about 7 percent above us-east-2's. The kept runs' tokens were counted on gpt-oss-20b and Gemma 4 12B, and W1's golden set also ran on Nova Lite (book D-033), so the lab measures how the counts differ on the same cases, input and output apart, and states the difference beside every priced estimate.
- **O-D6. Allocating what isn't a call (fig-16-02).** Direct costs are the cost lines, summed by run, workflow, and month. The self-hosted GPUs' hours are split among the workflows that used the alias by their share of its tokens that month; the idle remainder stays on a line of its own, which the platform team owns. The platform's fixed costs are split by each workflow's share of gateway calls and shown apart from the direct costs its owner controls. They are the gateway's database, Redis, and compute, Langfuse's stores, the shared endpoints of chapters 6 to 8, and the two licenses, at the vendor's published price or as a parameter where none is published. Each month the sum is reconciled with the bill at the bill's own grain, usage type per day by tag. For one real reconciliation, step 7 reads the lab account's Bedrock spend for the days of the lab's Nova Lite calls from Cost Explorer and sets it against their cost lines. The chapter prints no price (style guide 12.4), only ratios and percentages of the manifests' budgets; the prices stay in the dated table.
- **O-D7. Budgets, the alarm, and the kill switch.** The monthly budget counts in LiteLLM's database on a virtual key per workflow, as chapter 5 says, with PostgreSQL run on the workstation from its portable binaries. At 80 percent an alert goes to the owner and FinOps; at 100 percent the key stops. Step 1 settles which of these LiteLLM's MIT core provides (key budgets with a duration, soft-budget alerts, blocking a key). For anything that needs the enterprise license, the callback enforces it from the ledger, and the chapter says which. The kill switch has two halves. At the gateway, the workflow's key is blocked, by LiteLLM's key block if the MIT core has it and otherwise by a per-workflow state the callback reads on every call; neither needs a restart. At the identity, chapter 6's deny-all policy goes on the harness role and the tool roles. A drill measures both: a workflow loops on calls, the switch is pulled, and the lab records the seconds until the gateway's first refusal and, in the cloud session, until the first AccessDenied on credentials already issued. The rung-down switch lives beside it, as an override the harness reads at the start of each run, which can only lower the manifest's rung.
- **O-D8. Quotas and rate limits, measured against fakes.** A fake provider enforces a tokens-per-minute quota the way the fact sheet describes the three clouds': an estimate taken up front from the prompt and `max_tokens`, settled after the response, and a 429 with Retry-After when the quota runs out. The lab sends W2's and W3's traffic through the gateway against it. It measures whether a requests-per-minute limit alone lets a burst of large requests starve the incident workflow, and whether a tokens-per-minute limit per workflow, dividing the account's quota, keeps the incident workflow's share. It compares retries that honor Retry-After, with jitter and a cap on attempts, against naive retries, counting calls per successful request. It also measures the gateway's own latency per call. Load on the local model reuses chapter 9's measurements.
- **O-D9. Provisioned against on-demand.** A calculator in the manner of chapter 9's crossover works from the dated table: Bedrock's Reserved tier (the fact sheet finds Provisioned Throughput for no current Claude model; Nova's Model Units are checked in step 1), Azure's PTU with the sizing formula the fact sheet quotes, and Google's GSU with its per-model burndown. Its inputs are a workflow's tokens per minute in its busy hour and its duty cycle, and its output is the utilization above which committed capacity costs less, as a ratio. It answers chapter 8's question and sizes the incident workflow's capacity during an incident. The calculator gives the break-even; the chapter weighs it against Larkspur's bursty traffic and the GPUs chapter 9 already holds for Restricted data.
- **O-D10. Outage fallback and continuity, timed.** Chapter 5 built the fallback rules; this lab times them. With the fake primary failing, it records how long the gateway keeps sending to it before cooling it down (step 1 checks LiteLLM's defaults for `allowed_fails` and `cooldown_time`), what a run in flight sees, and that the fallback stays inside the manifest's Regions: W3 gets its 503 and W2 its fallback. A run lost in a failover fails and starts again, so the drill checks what stops the second run from repeating a write. A table lists each platform component (the gateway, its database, Langfuse, the audit log, the self-hosted path, W5's ledger) with its failure mode, a recovery objective, and the evidence. The chapter describes the second gateway Region of month five; the lab doesn't deploy one.
- **O-D11. Cross-region inference against residency.** The chapter argues this from chapter 2's grid and chapter 6's profiles, and the lab makes two checks. The gateway's rules refuse a profile whose Region list includes one the data class may not reach, read from the profile's own list as chapter 6 requires. And in the cloud session, a call through the US profile from us-east-2 has its serving Region read from the invocation log and CloudTrail and matched to its cost line.
- **O-D12. SLOs for agents (fig-16-03).** Each workflow gets three SLIs, computed from the cost lines and traces. Success counts runs that end in an answer, a correct refusal, or a proposal, over valid runs; a budget stop and a timeout are bad events. Latency counts runs under the workflow's threshold: seconds for W2's chat, the whole night for W5. An outcome SLI counts what a person decided: W4's and W5's proposals accepted, and W3's actions approved and not reverted. Quality from chapter 15's sets gates a release and has no SLO. The controls (residency, the never-list, the budgets) must hold every time, so they get no error budget. Error budgets and the SRE workbook's multiwindow burn-rate alerts (14.4 over one hour, 6 over six hours, 1 over three days) run over a simulated month built from the kept runs' distributions, with a provider outage, a throttled hour, and a model regression injected. The month is marked simulated wherever it appears. It also shows where burn rates fail. W5 runs once a night, so its alert counts failed nights, and a golden case sent through the gateway every hour gives the low-volume workflows a signal, at a token cost the ledger records. When a workflow spends its error budget, changes to its prompts and models stop; when it spends the outcome SLI's budget, it also moves down a rung.
- **O-D13. On-call and the misbehaving-agent playbook.** A runbook in the companion and a table in the chapter: symptom, signal, first lever, second lever, who pulls it. The symptoms are specific to agents: a loop that exhausts its step budget, a cost spike, a run of refusals or tool errors, a planted text restated (chapter 15's red-team signal), a denied egress (chapter 4's exfiltration through rendered output), confident wrong answers reported by people, a provider outage, and a model that changed under its alias. The levers are the rate in the manifest, the alias remapped back, a rung down, and the kill switch, each with who may pull it (the on-call lead on Tomasz's rota) and the change record it leaves (ch02:163). O-D12's fast burns page; everything else opens a ticket.
- **O-D14. The model lifecycle and the upgrade path (fig-16-04).** One register covers the three clouds and the local models, merged from chapters 6 to 9, with stage, retirement date, replacement, and the pinned identifier: a digest for a local model, and for Claude on Bedrock an ID with no version in it, a limit the chapter states. The 90-day rule of chapters 7 and 8 becomes an alert. The upgrade path runs once for real: a candidate behind the alias on a staging gateway, chapter 15's live job against the baseline, then a canary weight in the gateway for a share of one workflow's calls, watched against O-D12's SLIs, and finally promotion or a remap back. The candidate is a local model, so the run costs nothing. It waits for chapter 15's step 7.
- **O-D15. The monthly review with Ingrid.** A generator writes a month's review from the ledger, the SLOs, the budget events, the switches pulled, the register, and W5's ledger. Per workflow it shows runs, cost per run (median and 95th percentile) against the per-run budget, the month against the monthly budget, the shared lines, the error budget left, incidents, models within 180 days of retirement, and W5's proposed, merged, and realized savings with what finding them cost. Per-run figures are measured. Monthly volumes are Larkspur's estimates (chapter 9 gives W2's, 100 million tokens a month), and the review marks which is which. The chapter prints the review's shape and its percentages.
- **O-D16. Where AWS, Google, and Microsoft differ, offline.** From the fact sheet: what each observability service stores and for how long (Transaction Search indexes 1 percent of spans by default; Google keeps prompts out of spans; Application Insights moved GenAI content to `AppGenAIContent` on 30 September 2026); budgets and anomaly alerts; what a 429 means on each; the committed-capacity units; the routers' fallbacks (AgentCore Gateway's model fallback of 22 October 2026, API Management's backend pools, Google's model routing in preview); the SLAs, none of which covers Google's agent runtime; and the model retirement notice periods. Phoenix and OpenInference are named beside Langfuse.
- **O-D17. Out of scope:** dashboards in Grafana or a vendor console (the book prints no console); chargeback, which is chapter 18's; a second gateway Region deployed; anomaly detection by a model; a paging service (the alert rules print what would page); FOCUS exports beyond naming them; buying committed capacity; PII in traces and legal hold beyond the collector dropping content, which belong to chapter 17.
- **O-D18. The chapter's four figures.** fig-16-01: one W2 run's trace, from a real recorded trace, with the stores its trace id joins. fig-16-02: the cost allocation of O-D6. fig-16-03: the SLO dashboard sketch for one workflow, from the simulated month. fig-16-04: the upgrade path of O-D14.

## What the fact sheet's addendum changed (3 November 2026)

The addendum is section 8 of chapter 16's fact sheet. A script checked its 407 quoted fragments and 51 code spans
against the saved pages, and none failed. The facts amend twelve decisions, and each amendment below replaces the
matching part of the plan above.

- **O-D2.** CloudTrail records a session tag only on the AssumeRole event, and a later event "might not" carry the access key ID, so the plan's join through that key can break. The trace id therefore also goes into the role session name, which every later event shows inside `userIdentity.arn`. The name is the engineer's object ID, a dot, and the trace id's first 16 hex characters: 53 of the 64 characters STS allows, so chapter 6's session name still starts with the person. The full trace id stays a session tag on the AssumeRole event. The trust policy requires both shapes with `StringLike` patterns, since IAM can't require hexadecimal. `join.py` matches the name inside the ARN and uses the access key only when it is present. On the model side, `requestMetadata` reaches the invocation log only while logging is on in that Region, and the AWS docs leave unclear whether a text call is logged with text delivery off. Step 7 runs once with text delivery off and once with it on, on synthetic data. The serving Region comes from CloudTrail's `additionalEventData.inferenceRegion`, and the match between the invocation log's request ID and CloudTrail's is measured.
- **O-D3.** Pin the contrib Collector v0.164.0 and check the Windows tarball against its published SHA-256. The exporter is `otlp_http` now, and `otlphttp` is a deprecated alias. Tail sampling waits 30 seconds per trace by default, which the drills' timings allow for. The `file` exporter is alpha, so the chapter doesn't lean on its format. Transaction Search is account-wide: step 7 turns it on in a step of its own and waits about ten minutes before looking for spans. Its teardown sets the destination back to `XRay`, deletes the resource policy and `aws/spans`, and resets the indexing rule, and `leftovers.py` checks all four.
- **O-D4.** Langfuse v4.74.0, with the Compose file's images pinned by version, since it uses floating `:4` tags. Langfuse asks for 4 cores and 16 GiB (a t3.xlarge), and t3.xlarge isn't one of the free plan's six free-tier-eligible types. No page says the plan refuses it, so the first apply finds out, at about 17 US cents an hour from credits. The Collector sends `x-langfuse-ingestion-version: 4`, or new data can take ten minutes to show. Ingested cost beats Langfuse's own estimate, so the gateway's span carries `gen_ai.usage.cost` beside `larkspur.cost_usd`. The self-hosted alias gets a model definition through Langfuse's Models API.
- **O-D5.** LiteLLM's map has no `ca.amazon.nova-lite-v1:0` entry. A Canada-profile deployment without custom prices therefore gets no cost at all, and names prefixed with a Region fall back to the base price. Every deployment in the dated table gets `input_cost_per_token` and `output_cost_per_token`, and ca-central-1's 6.7 percent comes first in the comparison. The proxy fetches the main branch's map at startup, so the comparison runs with `LITELLM_LOCAL_MODEL_COST_MAP=True`.
- **O-D6.** The dated table takes section 8.8's prices; the two licenses stay parameters, since neither vendor publishes one. Cost Explorer can't be enabled by API. The author opens it once in the lab account's console at least a day before step 7, or the reconciliation is skipped and the chapter says so. The query groups by `USAGE_TYPE` per day and filters on the product attribute `model`, at one US cent a request.
- **O-D7.** In LiteLLM's MIT core, with its database: key budgets with a monthly reset (on the 1st, in a timezone set to America/Toronto), soft-budget alerts by webhook or Slack, and `/key/block`. Email alerts, key tags, and tag budgets need the license, so the 80 percent alert is a webhook the lab receives and forwards, and the workflow's name goes in the key's alias and metadata. A spent budget answers 422, not 429, and the harness counts it as a budget stop. Every one of these needs the database, and on native Windows LiteLLM 1.106.6's Prisma watcher kills its own query engine (an open issue and two open pull requests). Step 4 therefore opens with a smoke test, with `prisma` added to the requirements. If the engine dies, the database drills run on the cloud session's Linux VM, and the gateway's own callback stays the fallback. IAM documents only eventual consistency, and its revoke flow allows about 30 seconds, so the deny-all drill's number is one account's measurement on one day. The revoke-older-sessions policy on `aws:TokenIssueTime` is the documented way to cut off credentials already issued.
- **O-D8.** LiteLLM's key-level TPM limiter reserves an estimate before the call and settles it after, as the clouds' quotas do, so it is a second subject beside the fake provider. Deployment `rpm` and `tpm` apply only with `enforce_model_rate_limits`, and they also cap concurrency per process with an immediate refusal, so the lab sets `max_parallel_requests` explicitly.
- **O-D9.** Nova Lite has no committed capacity in Canada: Provisioned Throughput is sold only in us-east-1, and the Reserved tier isn't offered for it. The only Reserved tier priced in ca-central-1 is Claude Sonnet 4.5's, for geographic cross-Region inference. The calculator's Bedrock line is that tier, with Nova Lite's us-east-1 units beside it as the grid's counterexample. Azure's regional PTU in Canada East (50 PTUs at the least) and Google's GSU terms are the other two.
- **O-D10.** Chapter 5's config has one deployment per alias. With LiteLLM's defaults, such a deployment is never cooled down by a 429, nor by 5xx errors below 1,000 requests a minute. The drill sets `allowed_fails` explicitly and to a value other than 3, which 1.106.6 treats as unset, and sets `cooldown_time` too. Retry-After is honored up to 60 seconds, and only when no other deployment in the group is healthy.
- **O-D11.** AWS's page on the newer sign-up says cross-Region features are "not supported" on its plan, while chapter 6 measured the Canada profile working in this account. Step 7's US-profile call is therefore a test, and a refusal would be a finding.
- **O-D12 and O-D16, smaller.** The SRE workbook backs both of O-D12's moves: the hourly probe is its "artificial traffic", and W5's failed nights its longer window. The simulated month adds a real failure that a green probe hides, and a workflow that fails completely inside a group. For O-D16, Google's inference request metadata logging went GA on 30 October 2026: one log entry per request with the model, Region, and tokens, never the prompt. It is the counterpart of Bedrock's invocation log, and the comparison names it.

## Settled from the cost ledger's results (3 November 2026)

The ledger (`ledger/`) is built from the runs the workflows already kept, with no model. Two questions it
answered:

- **O-D6, amended: the platform's fixed costs are split in proportion to tokens, as chapter 2 already says** ("shared across workflows in proportion to tokens. That is crude and sufficient", ch02:144). The ledger shows the two rules give nearly the same shares, so chapter 2 stands as written. Those costs are about 467 US dollars a month at the table's prices. They land mostly on W2, almost twice its monthly budget, so the chapter shows them as showback beside the direct costs the budget governs, never against the budget.
- **The self-hosted alias's tenants.** The manifests list it for W1, W2, and W3. In the kept runs only W1 and W3 sent it calls, and W2, whose data is Internal at most, never did. Chapter 16 states the three manifest tenants and splits the GPU hours by each month's measured tokens, with the idle remainder on the platform team's line. The GPUs are over 99.96 percent idle on either reading, so without that line W1 alone would carry 25 times its monthly budget. Chapter 9's "for two workflows" (ch09:18) and "Two workflows share the replicas" (ch09:200) predated chapter 10's Restricted calls from month six, and chapter 9 was corrected.
